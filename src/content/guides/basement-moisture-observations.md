## First decide whether it is safe to enter

**Do not enter a flooded basement** or approach water near electrical panels, outlets, appliances, wiring, or suspected sewage. Avoid a room with an unstable ceiling, damaged structure, chemical odor or other immediate hazard. Keep others out and contact appropriate emergency or qualified services. Never reach through water for a breaker, sump pump or valve. Do not open a sump cover, remove wall panels, lift carpet, or run appliances to recreate moisture.

For active water, promptly contact a plumber, building professional or property manager as appropriate. The [EPA's flooded-home guidance](https://www.epa.gov/emergencies-iaq/text-version-flooded-home) explains why electrical, structural and contamination risks take priority over documenting damage. A safe photo from outside the area is enough; no photo is worth entering danger.

## The six-zone basement observation map

This is a **location-and-change map**, not a leak locator. Begin at the safe entry, walk only on dry stable flooring, and record visible signs without touching suspect materials. Label each photo with a zone and date; leave inaccessible zones blank.

| Zone | Visible signs to record | Useful context | What you cannot conclude |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Wall–floor joint** | Dark line, damp patch, water pooling or mineral-looking deposit | Which wall and distance from corner; recent rain or snowmelt | A wet joint proves a foundation crack |
| **2. Exposed foundation wall** | Staining, peeling finish, damp-looking blocks or discoloration | Height and width of mark; repeat photo angle | Discoloration identifies mold or the water pathway |
| **3. Basement windows / wells** | Water marks, damp trim or wet-looking sill | Weather and whether marks recur after precipitation | The window itself is necessarily leaking |
| **4. Visible plumbing / laundry area** | Drips, puddles or staining below visible lines and fixtures | Ordinary-use timing, without deliberately testing a leak | The nearest fitting must have failed |
| **5. Floor / stored materials** | Damp cardboard, warped flooring, staining or musty odor | Which items and whether changes spread | An odor establishes mold type or source |
| **6. Sump / drain vicinity, from a distance** | Water outside the pit, unusual visible pooling or repeated damp patches | Date, weather and any known service history | A pump is working or failing based on appearance alone |

The [EPA's building moisture guidance](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/moisture-control-guidance-building-design-construction-and-maintenance-0) addresses several pathways including site drainage, foundations and plumbing. These pathways can overlap; a visual observation alone does not distinguish them.

## Three observations that make the map useful

### On discovery

From a safe position, record the date and time, zone, whether water is visibly active, and the approximate area affected (for example, “a patch about one floor tile wide”). Take one context photo and one detail photo without moving electrical equipment or touching growth. If you already own a humidity meter, you may note the room reading and where it was taken. One reading cannot determine whether water entered through a wall.

### After ordinary weather or use

If conditions remain safe, take a second photo from the same location. Note rain, snowmelt, warm humid weather, and normal laundry or plumbing use since the previous observation. **A correlation is a clue, not proof.** Do not intentionally run water, pour water into drains, open a wall or test a pump to reproduce a suspected problem.

### At professional handoff

Share the map, photo timestamps, changes, known history and safety concerns. Ask which source was confirmed, whether hidden materials require assessment, and what follow-up evidence to record. A plumber, building-envelope specialist or qualified restoration professional may address different parts of the problem. In a shared or managed building, contact the property manager according to building rules.

## Which signs change the next action?

| Observation | Next step |
| --- | --- |
| Old, unchanged mark with no active water observed | Record and ask about past repairs; do not declare it resolved |
| New musty odor, recurring dampness or swelling | Arrange timely qualified assessment and track changes from safe areas |
| Visible water entering, spreading wet materials or ongoing drip | Prompt professional help to control the source and assess damage |
| Floodwater, electricity, sewage, chemical contamination or structural concern | Stay out and contact appropriate emergency/qualified services |

The [EPA mold and moisture guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) emphasizes fixing the water source and promptly drying affected materials. Its general 24–48-hour drying discussion is **not a waiting period** for an active leak or a guarantee against mold. The EPA advises keeping indoor relative humidity below 60%, ideally 30–50%, where feasible; humidity is only one part of moisture assessment.

## Two hypothetical examples

**After a storm:** A homeowner notices a new wet-looking strip at the wall–floor joint after heavy rain. They record the location, weather and photographs, and arrange an assessment. They do not assume the foundation wall is the source.

**During humid weather:** Another homeowner notices a musty smell and damp-looking stored cardboard, with no visible water entry. They note room humidity if a meter is available, photograph from a safe distance and seek help for recurring dampness. They do not label the material as a particular mold or assume a dehumidifier will repair a hidden leak.

These examples are fictional, not site inspections.
