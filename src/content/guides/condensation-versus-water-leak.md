## Start with the location of the water

The same window can have more than one moisture problem. **Do not diagnose from one photograph or a single rainy day.** Use this original comparison as a note-taking aid, not a test that rules out hidden water damage.

| What you can observe safely | One possible explanation | What to record next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fine droplets or fog on the **room-facing glass**, especially on cold mornings or after showers/cooking | Indoor humidity meeting a cool surface (condensation) | Room, time, indoor humidity if measured, outdoor conditions, and whether droplets recur |
| Wet trim, a localized wall stain, or water appearing during or after wind-driven rain | Possible water entry around the window, wall, flashing or another path | Rain and wind timing, exact first wet spot, whether stain spreads, photos from the same indoor position |
| Haze apparently **between sealed glass panes**, not on either accessible face | Possible insulated-glass seal problem rather than ordinary room-side condensation | Which pane, whether the haze persists, window age or model if known; ask a window professional |
| Damp sill beneath obvious glass condensation | Condensation runoff **or** a separate water-entry issue | Whether the glass was wet first, extent of damp material, and recurrence after rain |
| Persistent wet wall, musty odor or spreading discoloration | A moisture problem requiring timely investigation, regardless of source | First noticed date, size and changes; arrange qualified assessment |

The [EPA's moisture guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says condensation can signal high humidity and emphasizes addressing the water source. [BC Housing's window and door maintenance guide](https://www.bchousing.org/publications/Maintenance-Matters-13-Window-Door-Replacement.pdf) distinguishes surface condensation from water leakage and describes between-pane fogging. Neither reference allows a homeowner to rule out concealed damage by appearance alone.

## A safe three-moment observation

### 1. At first discovery

From a dry, stable indoor location, photograph the entire window and then the affected area. Note **which surface** is wet: interior glass, frame, sill, trim, wall or an inaccessible space between panes. Record the time, room and whether there is active dripping. Avoid touching suspect electrical equipment or opening wall or window assemblies.

### 2. After the weather changes

On a later safe occasion, compare the same area with the first photo. Did it recur after rain, during a cold morning, or following indoor moisture-producing activity? Record the pattern, including counterexamples. A rain correlation is a useful lead, **not proof** that the window itself is defective: water can travel within a building before becoming visible.

### 3. When deciding who to contact

If the surface stays damp, staining spreads, material softens, water actively enters, or the cause remains unclear, contact an appropriate window/building-envelope or moisture professional, or notify the property manager. Share the dated record rather than requesting a specific sealant repair before the source is known. If water is near outlets, wiring, a ceiling fixture or an unsafe structure, stay clear and seek urgent qualified assistance.

Do not climb outside, lean out of an upper-story window, use a hose to force water into an assembly, dismantle trim, or apply caulk over an unexplained wet area. Suspected mold or contaminated water also needs appropriate professional evaluation. The [EPA mold and moisture guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) recommends prompt action on leaks and wet materials.

## Two examples that should not be treated alike

**Hypothetical home A:** Tiny droplets appear on the room side of bedroom glass on several cold mornings. No surrounding wall stain is visible. The homeowner records indoor humidity and the morning conditions and observes whether moisture persists. This pattern may fit condensation, but the observation alone does not establish the whole window assembly is sound.

**Hypothetical home B:** A brown mark on the upper interior window trim becomes larger after two windy rain events. The homeowner takes dated photos from inside and asks a qualified professional to investigate water entry. Replacing the window or caulking one joint without diagnosis could miss another path. Neither example is a real inspection or expert diagnosis.
