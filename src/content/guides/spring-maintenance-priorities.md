## First decide which spring you are having

A calendar date does not tell you whether snowmelt has ended, heavy rain is approaching or cooling season is already underway. Use the conditions around your home to order your observations. These are **planning triggers**, not a diagnosis or a substitute for local forecasts, manufacturer instructions or professional assessment.

| Conditions you actually observe | First priority | Later, when conditions allow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Snow or ice recently melted | From safe ground, note runoff routes, pooled water, displaced exterior materials and new interior stains | Ask a qualified provider to assess suspect roof, drainage or structural conditions |
| Repeated heavy rain | Watch where downspouts discharge and whether moisture appears indoors | Document recurring patterns and seek drainage or building-envelope advice |
| Mild winter with little freezing | Focus on rainwater pathways, wind damage and any existing maintenance backlog | Check the cooling equipment's manual and service history |
| Hot or humid weather arriving early | Note condensate leaks, indoor moisture and cooling-system performance without opening equipment | Schedule manufacturer-recommended qualified service if due |
| Dry, windy spring | Note visible loose exterior materials and debris around accessible equipment | Plan appropriate professional exterior inspection and routine yard care |

**Do not climb onto roofs, use ladders to inspect gutters, approach downed electrical lines or enter flooded electrical spaces.** A visible concern can be documented from a safe location and referred to a qualified professional.

## A three-pass spring walkthrough

### Pass 1: Follow water, not the calendar

After rain or thaw, observe from a safe location whether water collects against the foundation, whether downspout outlets appear blocked or disconnected, and whether any previously dry interior area has a new stain. Photograph the same location with a date and weather note; do not infer that a stain proves a particular leak source.

The [EPA's moisture guidance](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) identifies moisture control, maintained gutters and drainage away from foundations as important ways to limit mold-related problems. Its [interactive mold house](https://www.epa.gov/mold/interactive-mold-house-tour) also explains why roof leaks and hidden water damage merit prompt attention. Active water entry, spreading wet materials, mold concerns or suspected hidden leaks call for timely qualified assessment, not a wait-until-summer checklist item.

Use the [ground-level gutter care planner](/tools/gutter-care-planner/) to capture observations without turning them into a ladder project.

### Pass 2: Compare the exterior with last season

From public paths or your own safe ground-level vantage points, note visibly displaced siding, fallen branches, broken screens, damaged handrails or changed gaps around exterior openings. Avoid touching unstable structures, electrical equipment or anything overhead. A photo taken from the same angle as an older photo is often more useful than a vague note that something “looks worse.”

| Observation | What to record | Sensible next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New ceiling stain after thaw or rain | Room, date, weather, size and photograph | Seek qualified leak assessment promptly |
| Water pooling by a wall | Location, duration after rainfall and downspout position | Ask a drainage professional if it recurs or enters the building |
| Loose or missing roof material seen from ground | Side of home and a zoomed photograph from a safe position | Contact a qualified roofing professional; do not climb |
| Loose railing or unstable steps | Location and whether the area can be avoided | Restrict access and arrange appropriate repair |
| Minor cosmetic wear without moisture or safety concerns | Photo and desired repair date | Put it into a planned, lower-priority maintenance list |

This table does not classify a building as safe or unsafe. If a defect could cause injury or significant water damage, prioritize qualified advice.

### Pass 3: Prepare the systems you actually own

Check the manufacturer's documentation and the maintenance record for your heating and cooling system. A spring date does not automatically mean every filter must be replaced or every system requires the same service interval. Use the [HVAC filter schedule planner](/tools/hvac-filter-schedule/) to record the interval appropriate to your equipment and household, and arrange professional work when the manual calls for it.

If cooling is used in your home, note unusual odors, water near equipment, visible damage or changes in operation without removing panels or reaching into moving or energized components. The [Department of Energy's air-conditioner maintenance guidance](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/maintaining-your-air-conditioner) discusses filters, coils and condensate drains; equipment-specific servicing and electrical work should follow manufacturer instructions and qualified guidance.

## The priority rule: water, safety, essential systems, then appearance

A spring list is easier to finish when every item has an explicit reason for its position. This is a **triage framework**, not a prediction of repair cost or urgency.

1. **Immediate hazard or active water intrusion:** keep people away from danger and contact appropriate emergency or qualified services.
2. **Recurring moisture or worsening damage:** record the pattern and arrange professional assessment without waiting for an arbitrary monthly deadline.
3. **Essential system due for documented service:** follow the equipment manual, existing service records and local conditions.
4. **Routine exterior care and cosmetic work:** schedule only when weather, access and the actual condition make it sensible.

A home in a dry climate with no freeze exposure may have little reason to perform a “thaw damage” inspection. A house with a basement and recurring spring runoff deserves more attention to water pathways than a cosmetic paint refresh. Renters and condo owners should document concerns and notify the responsible property manager or association rather than assuming permission to alter shared building systems.
