## Two clocks, not twelve identical checklists

A January move-in and a July move-in should not produce the same outdoor to-do list. Use **time since move-in** for the learning and paperwork sequence, and **local season or weather** for exterior, heating, cooling and storm preparation. This is a planning framework, not a set of mandatory maintenance intervals.

| When | One-time learning priority | Evidence to keep |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Month 1 | Read the inspection report; identify accessible water shutoff locations and emergency contacts without operating unfamiliar controls; inventory equipment manuals and warranties. | Inspection follow-ups, model numbers and contact list |
| Month 2 | Make a baseline of visible interior moisture and leaks in safely accessible areas; note existing stains rather than assuming their cause. | Dated observations and safe photos |
| Month 3 | Map your heating/cooling equipment and its manufacturer maintenance instructions; check which filter and service schedule actually applies. | Filter type, manual link and service history |
| Month 4 | Review the first utility bills and water usage for unexplained changes, allowing for weather and occupancy. | Bill dates and questions, not assumed diagnoses |
| Month 5 | Walk the exterior **from the ground** and note drainage, siding and visible gutter concerns when weather permits. | Locations to ask a qualified contractor about |
| Month 6 | Revisit the inspection punch list; distinguish resolved issues, monitored issues and professional work still to schedule. | A three-status issue register |
| Month 7 | Check the condition of visible supply hoses and accessible appliance areas without moving heavy appliances. | Appliance and leak observations |
| Month 8 | Review smoke/CO alarm manuals, replacement dates and test instructions; perform due user tests per the product instructions. | Alarm model and due dates |
| Month 9 | Recheck accessible areas for moisture changes and confirm any repairs have documentation. | Before/after notes only when actually observed |
| Month 10 | Review insurance documents and your home inventory; store sensitive information securely. | Coverage questions and updated inventory |
| Month 11 | Review spending and identify which recurring tasks need a professional appointment. | Costs actually paid and next-year estimates |
| Month 12 | Retire irrelevant reminders and build next year's personalized plan around real equipment and weather. | One usable annual schedule |

These are **illustrative month assignments**, not deadlines. Move an item earlier when a safety concern, warranty term or seasonal hazard makes it urgent. You do not need to wait until month eight to address a failed alarm.

## The season clock: schedule around conditions

| Trigger in your location | What to plan or observe | Do not assume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before your cooling season | Review the cooling system manual and arrange any specified professional service; note accessible airflow concerns. | That all heat pumps and AC units share a fixed service date |
| Before your heating season | Review heating and alarm instructions; arrange professional heating/combustion service when applicable. | That every home has a furnace or gas equipment |
| Before freezing weather | Identify exposed water-system risks and ask an appropriate professional about protection if unsure. | That every U.S. home needs freeze preparation |
| After major storms or leaf fall | From safe ground level, look for changed drainage, exterior damage or blocked visible downspouts. | That roof or ladder access is safe DIY work |
| During humid or rainy periods | Watch for recurring dampness, condensation or musty odors in accessible rooms; address moisture concerns promptly. | That a visible stain proves a specific mold type or hidden cause |
| In wildfire, hurricane or other hazard areas | Follow current local emergency-management guidance and relevant household plans. | That a national month-by-month list replaces local alerts |

The [seasonal maintenance builder](/tools/seasonal-maintenance-builder/) can turn relevant conditions into a shorter checklist. The [climate care selector](/tools/climate-home-care/) helps distinguish broad climate concerns without pretending to know your address or forecast.

## Worked example: move in during September

Suppose a homeowner closes on a house in September in a place where winter freezes are common. **September is month one**, so the first action is to gather manuals, inspection findings and emergency contacts. But fall preparation is also due *now*, not in the homeowner's hypothetical month ten. They can arrange appropriate heating service and discuss exposed-pipe risks before freezing weather, while moving the less urgent utility-bill comparison to a later month.

For a September move-in in a hot, humid coastal area, winter freeze tasks may be irrelevant. Moisture awareness and locally advised storm preparedness may be more useful. Neither example establishes a universal maintenance requirement.

## When to stop and get help

This plan covers **observation, organization and scheduling**. Do not climb roofs or ladders, open electrical panels, adjust gas equipment, work on refrigerant lines, disturb suspected hazardous materials or investigate concealed water damage based on this article. For an immediate gas, fire or electrical hazard, leave the area and follow local emergency instructions. If an inspection report or professional has flagged a specific hazard, prioritize that advice rather than the example table.

The [EPA WaterSense home maintenance guidance](https://www.epa.gov/watersense/home-maintenance) supports checking for and promptly addressing leaks. The [EPA moisture guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) explains why moisture control matters. The [U.S. Department of Energy home energy checklist](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/femp/home-energy-checklist) offers system-maintenance context; none of these sources prescribes this twelve-month onboarding sequence.
