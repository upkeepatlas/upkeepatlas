## First choose the condition, then the observation

This matrix is a **decision aid**, not a diagnosis or an instruction to approach dangerous areas. Observe only when conditions are safe; use official local weather and emergency alerts.

| Current winter condition | Safe observation from inside or ground level | What to record or arrange |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hard freeze or prolonged cold | Changes in accessible faucet flow, unusual indoor dampness, visible frost in normally conditioned rooms, heating interruptions | Note time, affected room or fixture, and local temperature warning; contact a plumber or heating professional promptly if a system may be failing |
| Snow or ice accumulation | From inside or a clear, safe position, note new ceiling stains, drips, visible drainage changes or damaged exterior elements | Photograph from a safe location and arrange qualified roof/drainage assessment; **never climb a snowy roof or ladder** |
| Freezing rain or high winds | Official alerts, blocked safe exits, visible fallen limbs or damaged service lines from a distance | Follow local emergency instructions; stay away from fallen wires, damaged trees and unstable structures |
| Repeated rain in a mild winter | New wall or ceiling stains, musty odors, pooling seen from safe windows or paths | Record the first occurrence and whether it follows rainfall; arrange moisture assessment if persistent |
| Heating or power outage | Whether the home has safe heat, working alarms, supplies and a local warming option | Follow utility and emergency updates; do not improvise combustion heat or run a generator indoors |
| No unusual exposure | Previously logged maintenance needs, manuals, upcoming professional appointments | Defer discretionary outdoor work until safe conditions return |

**Immediate hazards outrank the checklist.** If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, you suspect a gas leak, see a fallen power line, or face dangerous structural damage, leave the hazardous area and call emergency services or the appropriate utility. Do not investigate or repair it yourself.

## The three-pass winter observation routine

### Pass 1 — Safety and weather, before looking around

Check your local National Weather Service forecast, emergency alerts and utility notices. Identify a safe exit, a backup place to stay warm if needed, and who will contact the utility or property manager. FEMA's [winter weather guidance](https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather) covers preparedness and winter-storm hazards. The [U.S. Fire Administration's generator safety flyer](https://www.ready.gov/sites/default/files/2023-12/ready-gov_winterready_portable_generators_winter_storms_flyer.pdf) warns that portable generators must be used outdoors away from doors, windows and vents; never use a generator or grill as indoor heat.

If the household is renting or shares equipment with an association, record the concern and notify the responsible owner or manager. Do not access locked or unfamiliar equipment.

### Pass 2 — Inside, without opening systems

Walk through ordinary occupied rooms and note *changes*: a new ceiling stain, a damp window surround, a recurring musty smell, an unusual heating-system warning or a newly cold room. Record the room, approximate time and weather conditions. A stain or condensation is a clue, **not proof** of its cause. The [EPA moisture guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) stresses correcting the source of moisture rather than merely covering visible effects.

Check the manufacturer instructions and service record for your heating system. [ENERGY STAR's heating and cooling maintenance checklist](https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/heating-cooling/maintenance-checklist) recommends professional pre-season checks. Do not open gas, combustion or electrical housings to investigate. Use the [HVAC filter schedule planner](/tools/hvac-filter-schedule/) only for the accessible filter and the interval specified for your equipment and conditions.

### Pass 3 — Outside only when the route is safe

After a weather event, look from a window or stable ground-level position for visible changes in gutters, downspouts, siding, drainage and trees. Compare with earlier photos where available. Do not walk under ice-laden branches, approach wires, break ice dams, clear elevated gutters or climb roofs. The [ground-level gutter care planner](/tools/gutter-care-planner/) helps turn a safe observation into a service request.

A draft may suggest an air-leak issue, but do not seal ventilation or combustion-air openings. [DOE's winter energy guidance](https://www.energy.gov/articles/5-tips-help-you-save-energy-bills-winter) discusses windows and weatherization; evaluate actual ventilation and equipment requirements before planning repairs.

## Which observation needs attention first?

| Priority | Example | Appropriate next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency | CO alarm, gas odor, downed power line, structural danger | Leave danger and contact emergency services or utility |
| Prompt professional assessment | Active water entry, lost heat during severe cold, suspected frozen plumbing | Contact a qualified provider or responsible property manager; follow local emergency guidance |
| Document and follow up | Recurring condensation, new stain, persistent draft, visible drainage change | Record date and conditions; compare and schedule evaluation if it persists |
| Routine planning | Filter reminder, ordinary service record, spring work list | Check manual and add a task to your maintenance log |

This is a practical sorting tool, not a substitute for an inspection. If you cannot tell whether a situation is safe, do not approach it.

## Example: two different winter days

On a hypothetical freezing inland day, a homeowner notices weak water flow at an accessible sink and a heating alert. Their record should capture the affected fixture, alert and time; they should seek qualified help promptly rather than attempt pipe thawing or open the heater. On a mild rainy coastal day, another homeowner notices a new wall stain after several storms. They should document its size and timing and arrange a moisture investigation if it recurs. Neither example represents a real inspected property or a diagnosis.
